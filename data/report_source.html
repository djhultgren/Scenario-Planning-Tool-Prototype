Executive summary

NOTE: CURRENTLY IN DRAFT. WILL COMPLETE THIS SECTION ONCE SECTIONS 7&8 ARE COMPLETE.

We will spend around €4.8m acquiring face-to-face agency donors in Germany this year. Those donors should give us €11.9m over the next ten years, €7.0m in net income.

Many things shape a donor’s LTV, but two matter most: what we ask donors for, and who we ask.

What we ask for

€20 is the best acquisition value for three of our four agencies, on gross and net LTV alike. It costs more to acquire, but the larger gift and strong retention more than cover it: €617 net LTV over 10 years against €368 at €10. That holds within every age band we can measure, so it is not an effect of €20 happening to attract older donors. However, the picture is more nuanced when we look at other acquisition values, we sometimes see differing pictures across age bands. For the under 30s for example, an annual gift of €60 (shown in the report as €5 per month) nets €128, whilst a gift of €12–13 a month, nets us €100 over 10 years.

Who we ask

Age is the strongest single predictor of value. On the same €10 ask, a donor acquired at 65 or over is worth €793 over ten years against €341 for one acquired under 30. It is why the same €20 ask is worth €1,112 at Trust and €882 at Apollon: only 4% of Trust’s donors are under 30, against 40% of Apollon’s.

Interestingly, half of every under-30 we acquire comes from Apollon, and a quarter from Wesser - our two most expensive rates buying our least valuable age group.

The €20 fee cap

The cap at Trust and Activate saves us about €15,000 a year. Less than 1% of their donors are acquired above €20, and with no fee to gain the agencies have little reason to ask for more. We cannot prove from the data that the cap is the cause, but it’s worth noting Apollon, an agency without the fee cap, have proportionally acquired 4x the donors giving above €20 compared to Activate, and 10x compared to Trust.

Methodology

We take the donors our agencies acquired in Q1–Q2 2026, assume the same level of acquisitions in Q3–Q4 2026 and follow that group forward ten years, using how donors behaved 2022–2024 to allow us to factor in retention rates and upgrade rates. Costs come from the 2026 fee schedule, applied donor by donor at the acquiring agency’s own rate.

At the moment, acquisition strategy and gift values vary across the four agencies in Germany. Whilst we do look at an overall net LTV by using a blended CPA, it is also important to consider each agency separately, since each agency has different cost structures. The scenario modelling in section 7 will therefore look at how we can optimise income on an agency by agency basis.

The 2026 cohort is considerably older than 2022–2024, whom we model behaviour from — 44 years on average against 39, with 18% under 30 rather than 36%. The agencies also differ: Trust and Activate acquire donors averaging 47, Apollon and Wesser 38 and 39. Using historic behaviour unadjusted would understate this year’s donors, and comparing agencies without adjusting would credit Trust for being cheap when part of it is that they acquire older donors.

In the model, if Apollon moved donors to €20, we assume those donors stay Apollon’s age, they do not turn into Trust’s average donor age. Ultimately, we are assuming agencies continue to acquire at the current age levels.

We were not able to link back all donors to an agency, we had particularly low coverage for X agency donors [SN TO ADD AGENCY NAME]. We therefore built each agency’s gift mix and age profile from the donors we could attribute, scaling those patterns up to the full-year volumes.

Historic data was limited for non-monthly donors. Assumptions have been made about the behaviour of these donors and can be found in the appendix.

On the 10 year horizon - years 1 to 5 are observed based on historic donor trends. Years 6 to 10 are projected.

Retention, and how often people pay

Retention does not decline as we ask for more, which is what you might expect. In fact, donors giving €20 hold up slightly better at 12 months than donors giving €15 or €12–13.

€5 sits well above everything else, but this is because these donors are almost entirely €60-a-year payers, and annual payers retain better — it tells us more about how they pay than what they give.

It is worth noting that age moves retention far more than the amount does: within any single gift level, 12-month retention swings by around 30 points between our youngest and oldest donors.

Note: Confidence on the €12–13 price point is lower than others due to a low historic base.

TakeawayRetention holds up as the ask rises — €20 retains as well at 12 months as €15 and €12–13, and better by year ten. Age is what moves retention, not the amount, so a higher ask costs us very little in retention compared with who we are asking.

5The fee cap

The €20 fee cap is currently in place for two agencies — Trust and Activate. It currently saves us around €15k in agency fees (€11,955 for Trust, €2,879 for Activate). Today, 0.3% of Trust’s donors are acquired above €20 and 0.9% of Activate’s donors are above €20. In contrast the uncapped agencies, Apollon are acquiring 3.7% of donors above €20 and Wesser are acquiring 2.2% of donors above €20 (though note Wesser’s focus is already on donors in the lower bands). This suggests that the current fee cap is preventing us from acquiring donors in the higher bands.

We can compare below, the additional income raised by donors at higher gift levels and compare that to the extra agency fee we would be subject to pay. We can see that there is no gain on €20 > €25. We actually lose €3 net for Trust donors and €28 net for Activate. However — things get interesting when we look at the donors giving €30 plus — suddenly, the gain in extra income outweighs the additional agency fee. This is true for both Trust and Activate.

Caveat: The historic donor pool of the retention rates of donors giving €25 is small and the retention rates are unusually low. It is possible that the retention rate of €25 donors will be higher in future. We should treat the €25 donor trends as indicative.

Agencies without a fee cap acquire noticeably more donors above €20 than those with one. Can we ask Trust and Activate to test pushing higher gifts with the current fee cap in place? If the answer is no, because they won’t have the motivation to sell at that level, we should consider removing the fee cap. The expectation being that without it, we can acquire a higher number of donors at higher levels, resulting in a higher net LTV and net income. Removing the cap should come with a commitment by agencies to offer the higher amount and to report how often it converts. We should ask agencies to offer €20 and €30 price points to donors, since the additional income received from donors giving €25 is not enough to outweigh the additional cost.

Key takeawayAgencies without a fee cap acquire noticeably more donors above €20 than those with one. Can we ask Trust and Activate to test pushing higher gifts with the current fee cap in place? If not, we should consider removing it.

6Scenarios: Acquisition and Retention — what we could gain, and what we could afford to lose

Later in the report, we will look at different scenarios to explore how we can optimise net income. However this section will simply look to understand the trade-offs and what level of trade-off is acceptable before acquiring at higher values is no longer worth it, i.e. the breakeven points.

What we could afford to lose: acquisition

Disclaimer before reading: The figures quoted across the acquisition and retention sections here, should be viewed in isolation, e.g. we are saying we could accept a 20% drop in acquisition or a 7pt drop in retention — not both. It also assumes the fee caps stays in place. If fees increased, the numbers may shift though we wouldn’t expect a change in trends.

For the sake of argument, lets assume that each agency is going to acquire 1,000 donors at its main ask level, this will be our reference point.

We can see that for Trust, Apollon and Activate, we could accept a circa 20–40% drop in acquisition before the extra income is no longer worth it. The % depends on the increase in ask. For Trust for example, we could allow a drop of 20% if acquiring donors at €15 instead of €10, or a 40% drop if acquiring donors at €20 instead of €10.

The only exception is Wesser where high fees and high retention of current donors means an increase in price point either result in needing to acquire a higher volume of donors, or only around an 8–14% drop being acceptable. The €20 ask is an exception to this.

1. Move the ask up, agency by agency

€20 returns €654 in net LTV against €391 at €10 — and it does so within every age band we can measure, from under-30s through to 65+. This is the single clearest finding in the report and it does not depend on any modelling assumption we tested. Trust already place 31% of donors at €20; Activate place 2%. The gap between those two numbers is the opportunity.

2. Fix €12–13 before anything else

€12–13 returns €326 against €391 at €10, despite the larger ask, and takes 39 months to cover its own fee against 30 months at €10. It is the only rung on the ladder that pays back more slowly than asking for less, and it occupies 19% of the file. Activate acquire 72% of their donors there and Apollon 36%. Moving that volume to €15 or €20 is worth more than any other single change available to us.

3. Price the age mismatch into the negotiation

Half of every under-30 we acquire comes from Apollon, who charge ×24.01 with no cap, and a further 28% from Wesser at ×29.65. We are paying the most for the age group that stays the shortest. Either those rates come down, or the age profile those agencies acquire has to come up — the current combination is the worst of both.

4. Test the fee cap before removing it

The cap saves us roughly €15,000 a year. That is small next to the income sitting behind it, but the evidence that the cap is what suppresses higher-value acquisition is suggestive rather than proven — card design and the cap cannot be separated in the data, and only 109 donors sit above €20 in total. The sequenced approach in section 5 is the right one: ask Trust and Activate to test higher asks with the cap still in place, and treat a refusal as the answer to the question.

5. What to claim, and what not to

The ranking of gift amounts and of agencies holds under every stress test we ran — cutting tail retention by a fifth, removing upgrades, applying mortality, discounting, and truncating to five years. €20 came top in all of them. The totals are far less firm: ten-year net income moves between roughly €3.2m and €7.0m depending on those same assumptions, and 67% of net income arrives after month 60, where the data is projected rather than observed. Argue confidently about which ask and which agency. Be careful arguing about how much.

Appendix I — Assumptions

Volumes and donor behaviour
  • 2026 volumes. The first half is actual; the second half assumes the same pattern repeats. Volumes are scaled agency by agency to the realised-donor counts in the cost file, because the donor extract is incomplete and unevenly so.
  • Behaviour comes from the 2022–24 cohorts, weighted to the 2026 age profile. The 2026 cohort averages 43.8 years against 39.1 historically, so retention and LTV at each gift amount are re-weighted to the ages we are actually acquiring at that amount.
  • Everything is a monthly equivalent. Annual, quarterly and twice-yearly pledges are divided down. This is what the fee is charged on.
  • Nine groups are modelled — €5, €8, €10, €12–13, €15, €20, €25, €30 and a pooled “Other” — so every donor is included.
  • We should consider the €12–13 and €25 price points with caution — the behaviour of these donors has been modelled from a low base.
  • Upgrades happen within the first year. Where a donor’s latest gift is higher than their first, the increase applies from month 13. Downgrades affect about 1% of donors and are not modelled.
  • Retention beyond month 24 is age-specific. Each age band uses its own observed year 3, 4 and 5 retention, from the 2019–22 acquisition cohorts.
  • Years 6 to 10 are modelled, not observed. A curve is fitted per age band to the five observed years and projected forward. No mortality adjustment is applied, so the 65+ figures should be read as an upper bound.
  • The scenarios move people, not profiles. Donors moved to a different amount keep the behaviour of that amount at their own agency’s cost.
Costs
  • Each donor carries their own agency’s fee. Costs are not averaged across the four.
  • The €20 cap applies to Trust and Activate only.
  • Fee rates are taken from the 2026 cost file as supplied.
  • Historic behaviour cannot be split by agency. Differences between agencies come from what they charge and who they acquire, not from how their donors behave afterwards.
  • Acquisition cost only. Development costs are not included.
